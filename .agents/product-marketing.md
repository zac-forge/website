## Product Overview

**One-liner:** A senior technology studio that builds systems which outlive the engagement.

**What it does:** ZAC advises on what is worth building, builds the systems that follow, and stays on as senior capability when a client wants it. The work is operational software: the systems a business actually transacts on. Engagements end with the client owning the code and their own team able to run it.

**Product category:** Senior technology studio. Buyers arrive looking for a "software development agency," "custom software," or "fractional technology leadership," so those are the shelves, but the category is entered against a scar: an agency that left them with something nobody could maintain.

**Product type:** Services. Project and retainer.

**Business model:** Two productized entry points, then project or retainer.
- Backlog Review: 2 to 3 weeks, from $6,500, credited in full against a build started within 60 days.
- Two-Week Proof: 2 weeks, $5,000, fixed price.

## Target Audience

**Target companies:** Mid-market operators with a system the business depends on. Auction houses, field and event operations, multi-site service businesses, institutions with public-facing booking or transaction flows. Not startups building a first product, and not enterprises with a large internal platform team.

**Decision-makers:** Owner, COO, or the most senior technical person, often the only one. In smaller firms, the founder. Rarely a committee.

**Primary use case:** They own or need a system that has to work while a customer is standing in front of them, and either it does not exist, or it exists and nobody can maintain it.

**Jobs to be done:**
1. Build me something that will still work in five years.
2. Get me off a system that only its original author understands.
3. Tell me honestly whether this is worth building at all.

**Use cases:** Real-time transaction capture on site. Point of sale plus same-day fulfilment. Branded booking portals. Migration off proprietary software onto something the client can own. Second opinions on a vendor or an architecture decision. Fixing what someone else built.

## Problems & Pain Points

**Core problem:** The systems a business runs on are built by people who then leave, and what remains cannot be maintained, extended, or trusted.

**Why alternatives fall short:**
- Large agencies staff seniors on the pitch and juniors on the build, so the people who understood the decisions are gone before handover.
- Freelancers are cheap and single points of failure. When they move on, the knowledge does too.
- In-house hiring is a 95 day average time to fill for senior developers, and top candidates leave the market in under 10 days.
- Most vendors are structurally incentivized toward lock-in, because the handover is where their revenue stops.

**What it costs them:** A rebuild every few years, plus the operational risk of running on something nobody understands. The buyer literature is consistent that abandonment, not cost or delay, is the deepest complaint in this category.

**Emotional tension:** Having been the person who signed off on the last one.

## Competitive Landscape

**Direct:** Boutique and mid-size development agencies. Fall short because 89% position across 3 or more verticals, which reads as interchangeable, and because their staffing model separates the people who decide from the people who build.

**Secondary:** Hiring in-house. Falls short on time to hire and on the fact that one senior hire is still one point of failure.

**Indirect:** Off-the-shelf or proprietary vertical software. Falls short when the business does not fit the product, and it is the thing ZAC has migrated a client off before.

**Also indirect:** Doing nothing and running the process manually. This is the most common competitor and the hardest to displace.

## Differentiation

**Key differentiators:**
- Dated, checkable longevity. Systems built in 2010 and 2016 to 2019 are in production today.
- Documented anti-lock-in. Legacy Studios was moved off proprietary software onto open source, then handed to their own team to run.
- The person in the meeting is the person doing the work.
- One principal across 15 years and more than 100 engagements, so the judgment is continuous rather than reassembled per project.

**How we do it differently:** Handover is designed in from the start rather than treated as the end of the revenue. The client owns the code outright.

**Why that is better:** It removes the single largest risk in the category, which is being abandoned with something unmaintainable.

**Why customers choose us:** GAP. No win/loss data exists. This is inference from the case studies, not from buyers.

## Objections

| Objection | Response |
|---|---|
| How do I know you will not disappear like the last one? | Same principal for 15 years. Systems from 2010 still in production. The 2016 build was handed to the client's own team and still runs. |
| A small team is a risk. | The risk is a large team whose seniors rotate off after the pitch. Here the senior people are the delivery. |
| You are more expensive than a freelancer. | Compared against rebuilding in three years, not against an hourly rate. |
| We do not know what we need yet. | That is the Backlog Review, and it is credited in full against a build. |

**Anti-persona:** Anyone who wants a disposable prototype, a marketing microsite, or the lowest bid. Anyone who wants to buy a vendor relationship rather than own the code. Startups pre-product-market-fit who should be building something throwaway on purpose.

## Switching Dynamics

**Push:** A system nobody can maintain, an agency that went quiet, a manual process that has started costing real money.

**Pull:** Evidence that someone builds things that last, and hands them over.

**Habit:** The manual workaround works well enough, and replacing it is nobody's job this quarter.

**Anxiety:** That this will be the second failed attempt, and that it will be their signature on it again.
